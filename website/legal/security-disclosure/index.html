Security & Vulnerability Reporting

Version 1.0 · 2 October 2026

Operated by Vaylo Sports · Contact: vaylosportssupport@gmail.com

The short version. If you find a security flaw in Vaylo Sports, please tell us privately rather than publishing it. We will acknowledge your report, we will not take legal action against you for good-faith research, and we will credit you if you want to be credited.
On this page
  1. How to report a vulnerability
  2. Our safe-harbour promise
  3. What is out of scope
  4. What we do when you report
  5. A weakness in our own position

How to report a vulnerability

Email vaylosportssupport@gmail.com with Security in the subject line. Please do not use a public issue tracker, a social media post, or a public support form for a live vulnerability.

Useful to include:

  • what the flaw does and what an attacker could achieve;
  • the steps to reproduce it, or a proof of concept;
  • which part of the product it affects — app, website, or an API;
  • the app version or build number if it is in the app;
  • anything you have already done, including whether anyone else knows.

Plain text is fine. You do not need to prove exploitability, and you do not need to have read this page before testing.

Our safe-harbour promise

If you make a good-faith effort to report a vulnerability, follow the scope below, and give us a reasonable opportunity to fix it before disclosure, we will not pursue legal action against you.

"Good faith" means you are trying to help, not trying to cause harm. In practice that means:

  • you avoid accessing data that is not yours — no reading other athletes' records, no listing accounts you do not own;
  • you avoid degrading the service for other people — no denial of service, no exhausting quotas, no mass messaging;
  • you avoid persistence — no leaving backdoors, no planting anything, no modifying data;
  • you avoid privacy harm — you do not access or retain personal data beyond the minimum needed to demonstrate the flaw;
  • you give us a reasonable window to fix the issue before you publish technical detail;
  • you tell us plainly if anyone else already knows.

This promise covers you even if you are not a customer, and it does not depend on us agreeing that the flaw is a vulnerability. It is a commitment about how we treat people who come to us in good faith.

This is not a bug bounty programme. We do not run one, we do not pay for reports, and we are not going to imply otherwise. This page exists because we would rather hear about a flaw than not.

What is out of scope

These are not vulnerabilities, and we will usually close them without a response:

  • the app or website telling you what it already shows you — your own data, your own rank, your own settings;
  • a missing security header on a page with no sensitive content;
  • self-XSS, or a flaw that needs you to already control an account;
  • being able to see data that is deliberately public to signed-in athletes, such as a public profile or a leaderboard;
  • social engineering of our staff, or phishing;
  • the absence of a bug bounty;
  • reports generated by an automated scanner with no evidence of a real impact;
  • denial of service, even politely.

If you are unsure whether something is in scope, report it anyway. It costs you an email and it costs us ten minutes.

What we do when you report

We triage reports by how much damage they could do, not by how interesting they are. A flaw that exposes one athlete's health data outranks a cosmetic one every time.

What we will tell you: that we received it, our initial severity view, and whether it has been fixed.

What we will not do: commit to a disclosure deadline, pay a fee, or share the internal details of a fix while it is live.

We do not have a published response-time commitment for security reports, and we are not going to invent one. If a flaw is being actively exploited we will treat it as an incident, and our incident approach is described in our internal policies — which we do not publish in full, because a public description of how we detect and respond to intrusion is itself a map of our defences.

We will credit you in a release note or a public acknowledgement if you want it and tell us how you would like to be named. If you would rather stay anonymous, we will.

A weakness in our own position

We would rather state this than let you find it.

We do not currently operate a formal, always-on security response process. We do not run a public bug bounty, we have not published a security certification, and we have not had our systems independently penetration tested. A report from a careful outsider is currently one of the more valuable security inputs we get, and that is not a position we would choose.

We are describing that here because a disclosure policy that implies a mature programme would be a false claim, and a false claim in a security document is the worst kind.

If you think you have found something serious, keep telling us until you get a reply. A month of silence is a failure on our side, not a reason to publish.
