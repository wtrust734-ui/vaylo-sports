AI Training Advice Disclaimer

Version 1.1 · 8 October 2026

Operated by Vaylo Sports · Contact: vaylosportssupport@gmail.com

The short version. The AI features read your training log and suggest things. They are software guessing from numbers, not a coach, not a physiotherapist and not a doctor. They can be wrong in ways that look confident, and you should never change what you do because of them without checking with a person who can examine you.

Vaylo Sports is intended for adult athletes aged 18–30. That is positioning, not an age gate: age is not verified, and where an account shows an athlete is under 18 the age-based limits described in Child & Young Athlete Safety stay switched on. The guidance described here is written for the adult athletes the app is built for.

What the AI training features do

Vaylo Sports has features that produce a suggested training plan, a weekly review, or coaching-style feedback. They work by taking numbers from your own training log — sessions, volume, load, recovery, how you said you felt — and generating text from them.

Some of the underlying numbers are calculated, not measured. A load score, a readiness figure or a predicted limiter is arithmetic applied to what you typed in. If the input is wrong, the output is confidently wrong.

Which features use generated text, and which are plain arithmetic, is set out in the AI & Coaching Disclaimer. That page is the technical version of this one; this page is the part to read before you act on a suggestion.

What these features are not

The AI training features are not medical advice, physiotherapy, diagnosis, treatment, or coaching by a qualified person. They cannot examine you. They cannot see how you move, how you feel, or what is actually wrong. A person who has assessed you is in a different category from a model that has read a spreadsheet.
  • Not a diagnosis. Nothing here identifies an injury, illness or condition, and nothing here should be read as ruling one out.
  • Not injury clearance. A suggestion to resume or increase training is not permission from a clinician. Only a qualified professional who has assessed you can clear you to train.
  • Not a substitute for a coach who knows you, your sport and your season.
  • Not a replacement for load monitoring by a human who is watching how you actually respond.

For under-18s, the features that could generate nutrition or weight advice are switched off entirely, and training volume is capped by age. See Child & Young Athlete Safety.

Known limits, not hypothetical ones

These are the ways this kind of output goes wrong, stated before you rely on it:

  • It cannot see your life. It does not know you are ill, that you slept badly, that work is stressful, that you are growing, or that something hurts — unless you typed it in.
  • Garbage in, confident out. Log the wrong numbers and it will reason fluently from them.
  • It is only as good as what you told us. Incomplete, inaccurate or out-of-date information — an injury you did not record, sessions you did not log, a date of birth that is wrong — produces a suggestion that is wrong in the same direction. It does not independently know your health, your condition or what you are actually capable of. Never assume it has information you did not give it.
  • A model's own knowledge has a cut-off. Generated text can repeat guidance that has since changed, or standard practice that was never right for your sport, your event or your age.
  • It is confidently wrong. Generated text reads as authoritative whether or not it is right, which is the single most important thing to know about it.
  • It generalises from thin data. A suggestion from two logged sessions is not a training insight.
  • It does not know your history unless it is in the log.
  • It cannot see the future. Predictions are extrapolations with a wide error band, not forecasts.
  • It is not individual to you. The reasoning is generated from patterns, so it can reproduce the biases of the material behind it, including advice that is wrong for your age, your sex, your disability or your sport.

What to do instead of trusting it

  1. Treat it as a prompt, not an instruction. It is a suggestion to think about something, not a decision made for you.
  2. Check it against how you actually feel. If the numbers and your body disagree, your body is the better source.
  3. Change one thing at a time, so you know what worked.
  4. Progress gradually. If it suggests a big jump, that is a reason to be suspicious, not a reason to comply.
  5. Stop and get a professional if it suggests training through pain, illness, or a symptom. That is the point at which to ignore the app entirely.
  6. Do not use it to manage a medical condition. If you have one, the plan comes from your clinician.
Stop training and get help if you have chest pain, unexplained breathlessness, fainting, a fever with training, sharp or sudden pain, or pain that changes how you move. No app output overrides that, including output from this one. If it is urgent, contact your local emergency services.

More on reading the numbers and knowing when to stop: see Training & Health Safety.

Current availability

The AI features are not currently switched on in the production version of the app. They require an external AI provider, and we have not deployed one, so the features fail closed — they return an error rather than a fabricated answer. That is deliberate: a feature that cannot reach a model should say so rather than improvise.

This page describes what the features do and what they cannot do, so that the description is ready and accurate for whenever they are enabled — not as a claim that they are working now.

What is sent to an AI provider, and what is never sent, is set out in AI Data Use & Processing.
